1. Introduction

Our project builds a fleet of warehouse robots that learn to cooperate. Multiple robots must complete a queue of pick-and-deliver tasks without blocking each other, colliding, or entering corridors reserved for human workers. We implement QMIX, a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithm, and compare it against IQL (Independent Q-Learning) and a rule-based dispatcher at fleet sizes of 5, 10 and 20 robots.

The six papers below were selected because each answers a specific question we had to resolve before writing code. They are not a general survey of the field — every one is cited in at least one of our design documents, and two of them changed decisions we had already made.

A note on publication venues. In computer science and machine learning, the flagship conferences — ICML, NeurIPS, AAMAS — are peer-reviewed archival venues and are generally ranked above most journals in the field, with acceptance rates typically between 20% and 26%. This differs from disciplines where the journal is the primary venue.

We have nonetheless prioritised journal publications where one exists. Our selection is 3 journal articles (Journal of Machine Learning Research, IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, Applied Intelligence), 2 top-tier peer-reviewed conference papers (NeurIPS, AAMAS), and 1 non-archival paper (P3). P3 is flagged openly rather than presented as peer-reviewed; it is included because its empirical ablation study directly changed two of our design decisions. It is our sixth paper, so the five-paper minimum is met by peer-reviewed work alone.

3. Paper summaries

P1 — Monotonic Value Function Factorisation for Deep Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (QMIX)

Title explanation

A value function is a robot's estimate of how good a situation is. Factorisation means breaking the team's single overall value into one piece per robot, so each robot can act on its own share. Monotonic is the constraint that makes this safe: if any one robot's estimate goes up, the team's estimate can never go down. In short — splitting a team score into individual scores in a way that keeps the two consistent. The algorithm is called QMIX.

Objectives

To train robots that must each act using only their own local observations, while exploiting the fact that during training in simulation the whole system state is visible. The paper targets a gap between two extremes: VDN, which simply adds agent values together and is too restrictive, and fully centralised methods, which cannot be deployed on independently-acting agents. QMIX aims for a richer combination than a sum, while still guaranteeing that each agent greedily choosing its own best action reproduces the best joint action.

Methodology

Each agent has its own recurrent deep Q-network (DRQN) reading only its local observation history. A separate mixing network combines the agents' individual values into a joint value Qtot. The mixing network's weights are produced by hypernetworks conditioned on the global state and are forced to be non-negative, structurally guaranteeing monotonicity. The system is trained end-to-end by minimising a temporal-difference loss on Qtot. This scheme is called CTDE — Centralised Training with Decentralised Execution. Evaluation is on the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC).

Type of results achieved

In the paper: QMIX consistently outperforms both IQL and VDN across SMAC scenarios, with ablation studies isolating the contribution of the mixing network and global-state conditioning.

For our project: this is the algorithm we implement. It provides the exact network architecture for Week 7, and its monotonicity guarantee becomes a testable property — our automated test perturbs one agent's value upward and asserts the team value never decreases. We expect a task-completion rate of at least 80% at 5 robots, and a measurable advantage over IQL that we predict will widen as the fleet grows.

P2 — Benchmarking Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithms in Cooperative Tasks

Title explanation

Benchmarking means running many algorithms on the same problems under the same conditions so the comparison is fair. Cooperative tasks are problems where all agents share one goal, as opposed to competing. The title is therefore: a fair, like-for-like contest between MARL algorithms on problems where agents must work together.

Objectives

The authors observe that MARL lacks commonly-used evaluation tasks and criteria, which makes published comparisons unreliable. Their objectives are to systematically evaluate three distinct classes of algorithm, to provide reference performance figures the community can cite, and to release open-source tooling and environments so future comparisons are reproducible.

Methodology

They compare three algorithm families — independent learning (including IQL), centralised multi-agent policy gradient, and value decomposition (including QMIX) — across a diverse range of cooperative environments. Each algorithm receives its own hyperparameter search per task, so no method is disadvantaged by poor tuning, and results are reported across multiple random seeds. They open-source EPyMARL, extending the PyMARL codebase so implementation details such as parameter sharing can be configured explicitly, plus two new environments designed around coordination under sparse rewards — one of which, RWARE, is a grid-based robotic warehouse.

Type of results achieved

In the paper: reference performance tables across all algorithm–task combinations, and the finding that no single algorithm dominates everywhere. Critically, implementation details such as parameter sharing materially affect results — so comparisons that fail to control for them are not meaningful.

For our project: this validates our experimental design. It confirms QMIX-versus-IQL is the field's standard comparison rather than an arbitrary choice, and RWARE's existence establishes a grid warehouse as an accepted research environment. It also imposed a rule: QMIX and IQL must be held identical in network size, parameter sharing and training budget, so the mixing network is the only difference between them. Every such setting is logged for our results to be defensible.

P3 — Rethinking the Implementation Tricks and Monotonicity Constraint in Cooperative MARL

Title explanation

Implementation tricks are small, unglamorous coding choices — how rewards are scaled, how gradients are clipped, which optimiser is used. The monotonicity constraint is QMIX's core rule from P1. Rethinking signals that the paper challenges accepted wisdom. In plain terms: are recent improvements over QMIX genuinely better ideas, or just better code?

Objectives

Several published variants of QMIX claimed better performance by relaxing its monotonicity constraint. The authors set out to test whether the constraint is genuinely the limitation those papers assume, by separating the effect of the algorithmic change from the effect of code-level optimisations bundled into the same implementations.

Methodology

A controlled ablation study. The authors examine the released source code of QMIX variants, identify the code-level optimisations each contains, then apply those same optimisations to standard QMIX. All methods are re-run under matched conditions on SMAC and on Difficulty-Enhanced Predator-Prey (DEPP). They add a theoretical analysis of why monotonicity should help specifically in purely cooperative tasks, and open-source their implementation as PyMARL2.

Type of results achieved

In the paper: three findings. First, the reported improvements of QMIX variants are significantly affected by code-level optimisations rather than by relaxing monotonicity. Second, QMIX with normalised optimisations outperforms the variants designed to beat it. Third — contrary to common wisdom — the monotonicity constraint can improve sample efficiency in purely cooperative tasks.

For our project: two consequences. It warns that our from-scratch QMIX could underperform for reasons unrelated to the science, so we maintain an explicit checklist of these implementation details, each recorded, making any underperformance diagnosable rather than guesswork. It also corrected an argument we had already written down: we had assumed monotonicity would become more limiting as the fleet grows. Since our warehouse task is purely cooperative — the exact regime this paper examines — that assumption is contested and possibly backwards. We amended our design record rather than deleting the original claim.

Referenced journal or website

Hu, J., Jiang, S., Harding, S. A., Wu, H., & Liao, S. (2021). Preprint, arXiv:2102.03479; later presented on the ICLR 2023 Blogposts Track. Code: github.com/hijkzzz/pymarl2

Venue note (stated openly): this is the one item in our selection that is not a peer-reviewed journal or conference paper. We cite it for its empirical ablations, not as theoretical authority. It is retained because it materially changed our design, and it is our sixth source — the five-paper requirement is satisfied by peer-reviewed work without it.

P4 — Safe Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning via Shielding

Title explanation

A shield is a separate, non-learning component placed between an agent and the world; it watches proposed actions and blocks or corrects unsafe ones. Safe MARL means guaranteeing that no unsafe state is ever entered — including during training, when the policy is still bad at its job. The title reads: making multi-robot learning provably safe by adding a guard that does not learn.

Objectives

The authors note that MARL is increasingly applied in safety-critical settings requiring guaranteed safety during the learning process, and that current MARL methods provide no such guarantee. Their objective is to supply one, and to determine how it should be structured so it remains practical as the number of agents grows.

Methodology

Two shielding architectures are proposed and compared. In centralised shielding, a single shield is synthesised to monitor all agents' joint actions and correct any unsafe action. In factored shielding, multiple shields are synthesised based on a factorisation of the joint state space; these monitor agents concurrently, each responsible for a subset of agents at each step. Both are evaluated against unshielded MARL on safety violations, learned-policy quality and scalability in agent count.

Type of results achieved

In the paper: both approaches guarantee agent safety during learning without compromising the quality of the learned policies — safety is not paid for in performance. Factored shielding is more scalable in the number of agents than centralised shielding.

For our project: the strongest external validation of our safety design. Our human-corridor block is a factored shield in its simplest form — one shield per robot, whose correctness depends only on that robot's position and a fixed map of corridor cells. The first finding is direct evidence against a risk we had logged (that blocking actions might disrupt learned coordination), which we previously could only argue from first principles. The second is why we expect our safety check to remain fast at 20 robots. Our target is zero corridor intrusions across 20 dedicated safety-test episodes, enforced automatically on every build.

ElSayed-Aly, I., Bharadwaj, S., Amato, C., Ehlers, R., Topcu, U., & Feng, L. (2021). Proceedings of the 20th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2021), pp. 483–491. IFAAMAS.

P5 — PRIMAL: Pathfinding via Reinforcement and Imitation Multi-Agent Learning

Title explanation

Pathfinding here means multi-agent path finding (MAPF) — routing many robots to many destinations at once without collisions. Reinforcement learning is learning by trial and error; imitation learning is learning by copying an expert. PRIMAL combines both: robots learn to route themselves through a mixture of their own experience and copying an expert planner.

Objectives

The authors observe that most state-of-the-art MAPF planners rely on centralised planning and scale poorly past a few hundred agents, and that such approaches suit real deployments badly, where noise and uncertainty require paths to be recomputed online — impossible when planning takes seconds to minutes. Their objective is fully decentralised policies in which agents plan reactively under partial observability while still coordinating implicitly.

Methodology

Reinforcement learning is combined with imitation of an expert centralised MAPF planner, whose demonstrations are supplied during training. The authors emphasise careful reward shaping and environment sampling as essential to making the approach work. Agents observe only a local window of the world. Once learned, a single policy can be copied onto any number of agents, so the method naturally scales to different team sizes and world dimensions. Evaluation covers randomised worlds with up to 1024 agents, benchmarked against state-of-the-art MAPF planners, plus a hybrid simulation of a factory mockup involving both real and simulated robots.

Type of results achieved

In the paper: success rates competitive with state-of-the-art planners while scaling to 1024 agents, and successful validation on physical hardware in a factory mockup.

For our project: three things. It grounds the project in real robotics rather than a toy problem. Its central argument — centralised planning degrades as agent count grows — is precisely the mechanism we predict will cause our rule-based baseline to fall behind as we scale from 5 to 20 robots, which is the effect our scalability study is designed to measure. And its emphasis on reward shaping justifies our planned hyperparameter study treating reward weights as a research variable rather than a guess. One deliberate difference: PRIMAL trains one policy and copies it to any number of agents, whereas we train separately per fleet size to isolate how fleet size affects learning. PRIMAL's approach is better for deployment and is noted in our future work.

Sartoretti, G., Kerr, J., Shi, Y., Wagner, G., Kumar, T. K. S., Koenig, S., & Choset, H. (2019). IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, 4(3), 2378–2385.

P6 — A Review of Cooperative Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning

Title explanation

A review (or survey) paper does not present new experiments; it organises an entire research area, explains how the main approaches relate, and identifies open problems. Cooperative restricts scope to settings where agents share a goal. The title is therefore: a structured map of every major way researchers make multiple agents learn to work together.

Objectives

To present and organise recent MARL algorithms for cooperative problems, to explain the challenges each family of methods faces and how the literature has addressed them, to review real-world applications, to catalogue the simulation environments available for MARL research, and to propose future research directions.

Methodology

A systematic literature review rather than an experimental study. The authors organise cooperative MARL into five families: (I) independent learners, (II) fully observable critic, (III) value function factorisation, (IV) consensus, and (V) learn to communicate. Each family is explained in turn along with its characteristic difficulties and the mitigations proposed in the relevant papers, and connections are drawn between papers within each category. Separate sections cover emerging research areas, real-world applications and available environments.

Type of results achieved

In the paper: a taxonomy of the field, a comparative account of the strengths and weaknesses of each family, a catalogue of applications and environments, and a set of proposed research directions. Being a review, the contribution is organisational rather than empirical.

For our project: it locates our work precisely within the field. The two algorithms we compare are not arbitrary picks from a large space — IQL is the canonical member of family (I) and QMIX of family (III), so our comparison is between two of the five recognised approaches to cooperative MARL. This gives our project's framing an authoritative, peer-reviewed, journal-published source, and its sections on applications and environments informed our decision to build a custom grid warehouse rather than adopt an existing benchmark.

5. Identified gaps in this literature set

Our project also has a Responsible AI component covering warehouse-worker displacement, the ISO 3691-4 safety standard for industrial autonomous mobile robots, and energy consumption per delivery. None of the six papers above address any of these.

This is not an oversight. These are not machine-learning questions and no MARL paper will answer them; they require labour-economics research, occupational-safety literature, the standard document itself, and an engineering reference for robot motor power. Sourcing these is scheduled as a separate activity in Week 5 of our project plan, and recording the gap now is how we ensure it is not discovered late.
