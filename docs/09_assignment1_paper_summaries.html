1. Introduction

This project develops a fleet of warehouse robots that learn to cooperate. Several robots must complete a queue of pick-and-deliver tasks without blocking one another, colliding, or entering corridors reserved for human workers. We implement QMIX, a cooperative multi-agent reinforcement learning (MARL) algorithm, and evaluate it against Independent Q-Learning (IQL) and a rule-based dispatcher at fleet sizes of 5, 10 and 20 robots.

The six papers summarised below were selected because each one answers a specific question that had to be resolved before implementation could begin. They are not intended as a general survey of the field. Every paper listed here is cited in at least one of the project's design documents, and two of them caused us to revise decisions that had already been taken.

1.1 Note on publication venues

In computer science and machine learning, the leading conferences (ICML, NeurIPS and AAMAS) are peer-reviewed archival venues and are generally regarded as ranking above most journals in the field, with acceptance rates typically between 20 and 26 per cent. This differs from disciplines in which the journal is the primary venue of record.

Where a journal version of a paper exists, we have cited it in preference to the conference version. The final selection comprises three journal articles (Journal of Machine Learning Research, IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, and Applied Intelligence), two peer-reviewed conference papers (NeurIPS and AAMAS), and one preprint (Paper 3). Paper 3 is identified as a preprint wherever it appears rather than being presented as peer-reviewed work. It has been retained because its empirical findings directly changed two of our design decisions. As it is the sixth paper, the five-paper minimum is satisfied by peer-reviewed work alone.

3. Paper Summaries

3.1 Paper 1: Monotonic Value Function Factorisation for Deep Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning (QMIX)

Title explanation. A value function is an estimate of how good a situation is. Factorisation refers to dividing the team's single overall value into one component per robot, so that each robot can act on its own share. Monotonic is the constraint that makes this division safe: if any one robot's estimate increases, the team's estimate can never decrease. The title therefore describes a method for splitting a team score into individual scores in a way that keeps the two consistent. The algorithm is named QMIX.

Objectives. To train agents that must each act using only their own local observations, while taking advantage of the fact that the complete system state is visible during training in simulation. The paper addresses a gap between two existing extremes: VDN, which simply sums the individual agent values and is too restrictive, and fully centralised methods, which cannot be deployed on agents that act independently. QMIX aims to permit a richer combination than a sum while still guaranteeing that each agent selecting its own best action reproduces the best joint action.

Methodology. Each agent is given its own recurrent deep Q-network (DRQN), which reads only that agent's local observation history. A separate mixing network then combines the individual agent values into a joint value. The weights of this mixing network are produced by hypernetworks conditioned on the global state and are constrained to be non-negative, which structurally guarantees monotonicity. The complete system is trained end to end by minimising a temporal-difference loss on the joint value. This training arrangement is known as Centralised Training with Decentralised Execution (CTDE). Evaluation is carried out on the StarCraft Multi-Agent Challenge (SMAC).

Type of results achieved. In the paper, QMIX consistently outperforms both IQL and VDN across the SMAC scenarios, supported by ablation studies that isolate the contribution of the mixing network and of conditioning on the global state. For our project, this is the algorithm we implement. It supplies the network architecture we build, and its monotonicity guarantee becomes a testable property: our automated test increases one agent's value and asserts that the team value never decreases. We expect to obtain a task-completion rate of at least 80 per cent with 5 robots, together with a measurable advantage over IQL that we predict will widen as the fleet grows.

3.2 Paper 2: Benchmarking Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithms in Cooperative Tasks

Title explanation. Benchmarking means running a number of algorithms on the same problems under identical conditions so that the comparison between them is fair. Cooperative tasks are problems in which all agents share a single goal, as distinct from competing against one another. The title therefore describes a like-for-like contest between MARL algorithms on problems that require agents to work together.

Objectives. The authors observe that MARL lacks commonly used evaluation tasks and criteria, which makes published comparisons unreliable. Their objectives are to evaluate three distinct classes of algorithm systematically, to provide reference performance figures that the research community can cite, and to release open-source tools and environments so that future comparisons are reproducible.

Methodology. Three algorithm families are compared: independent learning (which includes IQL), centralised multi-agent policy gradient, and value decomposition (which includes QMIX). These are evaluated across a diverse range of cooperative environments. Each algorithm is given its own hyperparameter search for each task, so that no method is disadvantaged by poor tuning, and results are reported across multiple random seeds. The authors release EPyMARL, an extension of the PyMARL codebase that allows implementation details such as parameter sharing to be configured explicitly, together with two new environments designed around coordination under sparse rewards. One of these, RWARE, is a grid-based robotic warehouse.

Type of results achieved. The paper produces reference performance tables covering all algorithm and task combinations, and establishes that no single algorithm dominates across every setting. Significantly, it demonstrates that implementation details such as parameter sharing materially affect measured performance, which means that any comparison failing to control for them is not meaningful. For our project, this validates the experimental design. It confirms that comparing QMIX against IQL is the standard comparison in the field rather than an arbitrary choice, and the existence of RWARE establishes a grid warehouse as an accepted research environment. It also imposes a requirement on us: QMIX and IQL must be held identical in network size, parameter sharing and training budget, so that the mixing network is the only difference between them. Every such setting is recorded so that our results can be defended.

Referenced journal or website. Papoudakis, G., Christianos, F., Schafer, L., and Albrecht, S. V. (2021). Proceedings of the 35th Conference on Neural Information Processing Systems (NeurIPS 2021), Datasets and Benchmarks Track.

3.3 Paper 3: Rethinking the Implementation Tricks and Monotonicity Constraint in Cooperative MARL

Title explanation. Implementation tricks are small and unremarkable coding choices, such as how rewards are scaled, how gradients are clipped, and which optimiser is selected. The monotonicity constraint is the central rule of QMIX described in Paper 1. The word rethinking signals that the paper challenges an accepted position. In plain terms, the paper asks whether recent improvements over QMIX represent genuinely better ideas or merely better code.

Objectives. Several published variants of QMIX claimed improved performance by relaxing its monotonicity constraint. The authors set out to test whether the constraint is in fact the limitation that those papers assume, by separating the effect of the algorithmic change from the effect of the code-level optimisations bundled into the same implementations.

Methodology. The study takes the form of a controlled ablation. The authors examine the released source code of the QMIX variants, identify the code-level optimisations that each one contains, and then apply those same optimisations to standard QMIX. All methods are re-run under matched conditions on SMAC and on Difficulty-Enhanced Predator-Prey (DEPP). The empirical work is accompanied by a theoretical analysis of why the monotonicity constraint should be expected to help specifically in purely cooperative tasks. The authors release their implementation as PyMARL2.

Type of results achieved. The paper reports three findings. First, the improvements reported for QMIX variants are significantly affected by code-level optimisations rather than by the relaxation of monotonicity. Second, QMIX with normalised optimisations outperforms the variants designed to surpass it. Third, and contrary to the prevailing view, the monotonicity constraint can improve sample efficiency in purely cooperative tasks. For our project there are two consequences. The paper warns that a from-scratch QMIX implementation may underperform for reasons unrelated to the underlying science, so we maintain an explicit checklist of these implementation details, each of which is recorded, making any underperformance diagnosable rather than a matter of guesswork. The paper also corrected an argument we had already committed to writing: we had assumed that the monotonicity constraint would become increasingly limiting as the fleet grows. Since our warehouse task is purely cooperative, which is precisely the regime this paper examines, that assumption is contested and may be the reverse of the truth. We amended our design record rather than removing the original claim.

Referenced journal or website. Hu, J., Jiang, S., Harding, S. A., Wu, H., and Liao, S. (2021). arXiv preprint arXiv:2102.03479. Subsequently presented on the ICLR 2023 Blogposts Track.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2102.03479

This is the only source in the selection that is not a peer-reviewed journal or conference paper. It is cited for its empirical ablation study rather than as theoretical authority, and it is retained because it materially changed our design decisions.

3.4 Paper 4: Safe Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning via Shielding

Title explanation. A shield is a separate, non-learning component positioned between an agent and its environment, which monitors proposed actions and blocks or corrects any that are unsafe. Safe MARL means guaranteeing that no unsafe state is ever entered, including during training, when the policy is still performing poorly. The title therefore describes a method for making multi-robot learning provably safe by adding a guard that does not itself learn.

Objectives. The authors note that MARL is increasingly applied in safety-critical settings that require guaranteed safety throughout the learning process, and that current MARL methods provide no such guarantee. Their objective is to supply one, and to determine how it should be structured so that it remains practical as the number of agents increases.

Methodology. Two shielding architectures are proposed and compared. Under centralised shielding, a single shield is synthesised to monitor the joint actions of all agents and to correct any unsafe action. Under factored shielding, multiple shields are synthesised on the basis of a factorisation of the joint state space; these shields monitor agents concurrently, with each shield responsible for a subset of agents at each step. Both approaches are evaluated experimentally against unshielded MARL, measuring safety violations, the quality of the learned policy, and scalability in the number of agents.

Type of results achieved. The paper demonstrates that both approaches guarantee the safety of agents during learning without compromising the quality of the learned policies, so that safety is not paid for in performance. It further shows that factored shielding is more scalable in the number of agents than centralised shielding. For our project, this provides the strongest external validation of our safety design. Our human-corridor block is a factored shield in its simplest form, consisting of one shield per robot whose correctness depends only on that robot's position and a fixed map of corridor cells. The first finding is direct evidence against a risk we had recorded, namely that blocking actions might disrupt learned coordination, which we had previously been able to argue against only from first principles. The second finding is the reason we expect our safety check to remain fast at 20 robots. Our target is zero corridor intrusions across 20 dedicated safety-test episodes, verified automatically on every build.

Referenced journal or website. ElSayed-Aly, I., Bharadwaj, S., Amato, C., Ehlers, R., Topcu, U., and Feng, L. (2021). Proceedings of the 20th International Conference on Autonomous Agents and Multiagent Systems (AAMAS 2021), pp. 483-491. IFAAMAS.
https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.11196

3.5 Paper 5: PRIMAL: Pathfinding via Reinforcement and Imitation Multi-Agent Learning

Title explanation. Pathfinding here refers to multi-agent path finding (MAPF), the problem of routing many robots to many destinations simultaneously without collisions. Reinforcement learning is learning through trial and error, while imitation learning is learning by copying an expert. PRIMAL combines the two, so that robots learn to route themselves through a mixture of their own experience and imitation of an expert planner.

Objectives. The authors observe that most state-of-the-art MAPF planners rely on centralised planning and scale poorly beyond a few hundred agents, and that such approaches are poorly suited to real deployments, where noise and uncertainty require paths to be recomputed online. This is not possible when planning times are measured in seconds or minutes. Their objective is to produce fully decentralised policies in which agents plan reactively under partial observability while still coordinating implicitly.

Methodology. Reinforcement learning is combined with imitation of an expert centralised MAPF planner, whose demonstrations are provided during training. The authors emphasise that careful reward shaping and environment sampling are essential to making the approach work. Agents observe only a local window of the world. Once learned, a single policy can be copied onto any number of agents, so the method scales naturally to different team sizes and world dimensions. Evaluation covers randomised worlds containing up to 1,024 agents, benchmarked against state-of-the-art MAPF planners, together with a hybrid simulation of a factory mockup involving both real and simulated robots.

Type of results achieved. The paper reports success rates competitive with state-of-the-art planners while scaling to 1,024 agents, and successful validation on physical hardware in a factory mockup. For our project the paper contributes in three ways. It grounds the work in real robotics rather than a purely theoretical problem. Its central argument, that centralised planning degrades as agent count grows, is precisely the mechanism we predict will cause our rule-based baseline to fall behind as we scale from 5 to 20 robots, and measuring that effect is the purpose of our scalability study. Finally, its emphasis on reward shaping justifies our planned hyperparameter study, which treats reward weights as a research variable rather than a guess. One deliberate difference should be noted: PRIMAL trains a single policy and copies it to any number of agents, whereas we train separately for each fleet size in order to isolate how fleet size affects learning. The PRIMAL approach is better suited to deployment and is recorded in our future work.

Referenced journal or website. Sartoretti, G., Kerr, J., Shi, Y., Wagner, G., Kumar, T. K. S., Koenig, S., and Choset, H. (2019). IEEE Robotics and Automation Letters, 4(3), 2378-2385.
https://doi.org/10.1109/LRA.2019.2903261

3.6 Paper 6: A Review of Cooperative Multi-Agent Deep Reinforcement Learning

Title explanation. A review, or survey, paper does not present new experiments. Instead it organises an entire research area, explains how the principal approaches relate to one another, and identifies open problems. The word cooperative restricts the scope to settings in which agents share a goal. The title therefore describes a structured map of the major ways in which researchers make multiple agents learn to work together.

Objectives. To present and organise recent MARL algorithms for cooperative problems, to explain the challenges each family of methods faces and how the literature has addressed them, to review real-world applications, to catalogue the simulation environments available for MARL research, and to propose directions for future research.

Methodology. The paper is a systematic literature review rather than an experimental study. The authors organise cooperative MARL into five families: independent learners, fully observable critic, value function factorisation, consensus, and learning to communicate. Each family is explained in turn, together with its characteristic difficulties and the mitigations proposed in the relevant papers, and connections are drawn between papers within each category. Separate sections address emerging research areas, real-world applications, and the environments available for research.

Type of results achieved. The paper produces a taxonomy of the field, a comparative account of the strengths and weaknesses of each family of methods, a catalogue of applications and environments, and a set of proposed research directions. As a review, its contribution is organisational rather than empirical. For our project it locates the work precisely within the field. The two algorithms we compare are not arbitrary selections from a large space: IQL is the canonical member of the independent learners family and QMIX of the value function factorisation family, so our comparison is between two of the five recognised approaches to cooperative MARL. This gives the framing of our project an authoritative, peer-reviewed and journal-published source. The paper's sections on applications and environments also informed our decision to build a custom grid warehouse rather than adopt an existing benchmark.

5. Identified Gaps in the Literature Reviewed

The project also has a Responsible AI component covering the displacement of warehouse workers, the ISO 3691-4 safety standard for industrial autonomous mobile robots, and energy consumption per delivery. None of the six papers above addresses any of these subjects.

This is not an oversight. These are not machine learning questions, and no MARL paper will answer them. They require labour economics research, occupational safety literature, the standard document itself, and an engineering reference for robot motor power. Sourcing this material is scheduled as a separate activity in Week 5 of the project plan. Recording the gap at this stage is how we ensure it is not discovered late.
